About Eric Minish

Central Lake, Michigan. I build agent and data infrastructure, and I came to it through operational systems rather than through research.

I am most comfortable in the parts of a system where ownership, state, and follow-through start to get muddy.

Background

The professional work has been operational systems: workflow state, event handling, automation around CRM behavior, and the reporting layer that has to make all of it legible afterward. The part I care about is rarely the interface. It is the point where somebody needs to know what happened, what happens next, and whether they can trust it.

That question turns out to be the whole problem in AI infrastructure too, which is how I ended up here. An agent is a system that acts on state it cannot see directly, so the interesting work is in the layer that decides what it is allowed to know, what it is allowed to write, and what happens when a piece of that goes wrong. My knowledge graph takes proposals from agents and puts every one through review before it lands. The compression proxy in front of my tool servers forwards the original payload untouched the moment it cannot prove a perfect round trip. Neither of those is a clever trick; both are just refusing to let a convenient default quietly become a correctness bug.

I use these tools all day, which is the only reason I trust my own opinions about them. The homelab is the same argument in hardware — ten machines carrying real routing, deployment, monitoring, and recovery, so a claim about how something behaves under failure is something I have watched rather than read.

How I tend to work

Explicit ownership, real states with clear transitions, simple deploy and rollback paths. Monitoring that catches actual drift instead of producing decorative dashboards. Public copy that says exactly what is true.

The rule I hold hardest is that a system should decline to answer rather than fabricate one. A model in one of my pipelines is allowed to confirm or reject a candidate the deterministic layer found; it is not allowed to invent a new one. A board with no honest baseline to compare against returns null instead of a number that looks authoritative. A modeling target that turned out to be a restatement of a variable I already had got cut, even though the first test made it look like a win.

Most of the trouble I see comes from automation sitting on top of ambiguous state, or platforms built before anyone agrees what the workflow actually is. I try not to add to that pile.
