soundings keeps every published version of county economic data

Government economic figures are revised after they are first published, often for years. Most data services, FRED among them, show only the latest value. soundings is a store of US public economic data at county and township level that keeps every published version of each number, called a vintage, so it can answer what a series looked like on a given date.

What it is: a Python pipeline over Census Bureau and Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) data, plus past versions from ALFRED, the St. Louis Fed’s archive of FRED.

Status: private repository, started August 2026. Backtest runs from 28 October 2026; store counts from 3 November 2026.

What a vintage is, with one example

The store holds nine published versions of the labor force of Kalkaska County, Michigan, for June 2007. It was first published in August 2007 as 9,308 people. The April 2008 revision moved it to 9,285 and the April 2009 revision to 9,157. The latest version, from March 2025, is 9,121, which is 187 people (2.0%) below the first print.

Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Local Area Unemployment Statistics, via ALFRED. These figures were retrieved through the FRED® API; this page is not endorsed or certified by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis.

A warning signal checked against today’s revised figures uses corrections nobody had at the time, which can move it earlier or later than it was. The signal tested here is that residential building permits turn down before county employment does, a question regional economic-development staff ask when deciding where to commit staff or capacity.

How the store keeps versions

Revisions and corrections are stored differently

Every observation carries the period it describes and the date it was published, and a query can ask for a series as of any date. A publisher’s new number for the same month is a new row with a later date, and both rows stay readable. A mistake of soundings’ own is fixed by a row that names the row it replaces and gives a reason. Nothing is edited or deleted: data is written once as Parquet files (a column-based format) and read with DuckDB, an embedded database.

Seven stages with fixed permissions

Acquire, parse, normalize, store, align, detect and present. Only acquire may use the network and only store may write observations. No language-model call is allowed before present: one test scans for model-library imports, and another runs the pipeline with an empty environment, so no API key exists.

Every result names the kind of data it used

Each finding is labelled true_vintage (inputs as published at the time), latest_vintage (today’s revised figures) or mixed_vintage (one of each). Each input also records whether its publication date was stated by the source or inferred, and a true-vintage backtest refuses inferred rows.

Replay separates code changes from defects

Replay first compares a digest (a fingerprint) of the code, then reports one of four outcomes: code changed, so nothing runs; inputs changed, listed row by row; findings changed with identical code and inputs, which is a defect; or reproduced.

What is stored today 653 MB of observations

Five of eight registered sources hold data: Census county permits (11,369,010 observations) and place permits, BLS LAUS (Local Area Unemployment Statistics) county labor force (5,661,088), BLS QCEW (Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages) county employment (788,485), and ALFRED vintages for ten Michigan counties (93,254). The downloaded files take another 984 MB. There are 1,506 tests.

The detector under test

The divergence detector compares twelve-month growth in permits and in employment for each county. Each becomes a z-score (distance from normal in units of typical spread) against the county’s own previous 48 months, using the median and median absolute deviation, which outliers move less than a mean would. It fires when the permits score minus the employment score stays beyond ±1.5 for three months, permits have moved by at least 1.0, and employment has not turned.

Backtest: Northwest Michigan, 2004 to 2009

A backtest checks a detector against past data. This episode was committed before the detector was built: in ten Northwest Lower Michigan counties, permits fell well before employment. The detector is asked at every month-end from January 2005 to December 2009, 60 runs, reading only what had been published by each date. It passes if permits are reported first in at least three evaluable counties, with no exact month required.

In latest mode, employment is today’s BLS figures, cut off at the months released by each date. In mixed mode, from June 2007 (the first ALFRED version for these counties), employment is the version published by each date. Permits are the same in both.

“Reported” is the month-end run at which each signal first appeared. Lead is the number of months between the two reports.

Both modes pass, in 3 of 4 evaluable counties; in Benzie, permits never signalled. The modes differ in the employment columns. On today’s revised figures the employment downturn appears 3 to 6 months earlier than in the figures published at the time, so a backtest on revised data alone reports a lead of 12 to 14 months where the figures available then give 17 to 18.

Revisions also moved one signal. After ALFRED’s April 2009 revision of Kalkaska’s labor force, its permits signal is dated March 2006 instead of April. The backtest keeps the detection as first seen and reports the new date beside it.

National check on today’s figures

A single national run on today’s figures, with no month-by-month walk, scores every county. Of 507 counties the detector could evaluate between July 2005 and June 2008, 334 had a permits signal. In 293 the signal came before employment’s own downturn began, in 35 employment turned first, and in 6 no downturn appears by October 2009, the last month the walk could see. That is 293 of 334, or 87.7%. Weighted by each county’s employment, permits came first in 95.1% of the 328 counties where the order is decided.

The first version of this count gave 299, because it counted employment turns after October 2009 that no run in the walk could have seen. The national figure is context and does not decide whether the backtest passes.

What the evidence does not show

  • The permits data has no true vintages. Census publishes no revised county file, so none of the 11,369,010 stored county values has a second version, and the monthly files through August 2016 were re-uploaded in September 2016, so their publication dates are inferred. A true-vintage backtest is refused with a count of the rows that block it.
  • Employment vintages start in June 2007, so the 2006 permits signals were computed against today’s employment figures. ALFRED has no county employment vintages before 2019, so employment is computed from labor force and the unemployment rate.
  • The result rests on four evaluable counties, and the bar of three was met exactly. Of the other six, four lack monthly permits data, one has imputed permits and one has none. Before 2022 the monthly permits file covers only sampled permit offices, and nationally 83% of the 3,040 counties with both series are never evaluable in the window.
  • One episode in one region is the evidence. It says nothing about other regions or downturns, and it is not a forecast.
  • BLS published no county LAUS figures for October 2025, and under the original gap rule that one month blanked every county’s employment growth for 24 months; one missing month per twelve-month half is now filled. A 2026 change in population controls lowered LAUS county employment about 1% in 81% of counties, so the report flags affected signals and reads QCEW by default.
  • There is no CI (continuous integration, an automated test service); checks are run by hand.
